Lesson 001 LLM

From Pre-training to Alignment: The Full LLM Lifecycle

1. Context: Why NVIDIA Cares About the Full Lifecycle

NVIDIA doesn't just sell GPUs — it sells the platform for building LLMs end-to-end. The speaker, Giuseppe Fiameni, is a Solution Architect at NVIDIA who helps enterprise customers train and align LLMs using NVIDIA's software stack. His talk will almost certainly walk through the pipeline as implemented in NeMo Framework and Megatron-LM — NVIDIA's flagship tools for large-scale LLM development.

Understanding this pipeline matters for you because the JR1980206 role requires implementing DL models across domains including NLP/LLMs, and profiling their performance on NVIDIA hardware. You don't need to be an alignment researcher — you need to understand what the pipeline does at each stage so you can profile, optimize, and benchmark it.

Your existing mental model: You already understand distributed training (DDP, FSDP2, NCCL), mixed-precision (FP16/BF16), and scaling to multi-node. LLM pre-training is essentially this — but with additional parallelism dimensions (tensor, pipeline, sequence, expert) because models don't fit on one GPU. Post-training (SFT, RLHF, DPO) is what's genuinely new to you.

2. Stage 1: Pre-training

2.1 Objective

Pre-training produces a base model: a next-token predictor trained on trillions of tokens. The training objective is causal language modeling — predict the next token given all previous tokens. This is self-supervised: no human labels needed, just raw text.

The loss function is cross-entropy over the vocabulary at each position: 

L = -∑ₜ log P(xₜ | x₁, ..., xₜ₋₁; θ)

A base model (e.g., Llama-3 base, GPT-4 base) is not a chatbot. It's a completion engine. If you prompt it with "What is the capital of France?", it might continue with "What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Italy?" — because that's the most likely continuation in its training distribution. Making it actually answer questions is what post-training achieves.

Key Insight

Pre-training is where 99%+ of total compute is spent. A typical frontier model (e.g., Llama-3 405B) uses ~30 million GPU-hours for pre-training vs. ~thousands of GPU-hours for post-training. This is why NVIDIA's parallelism engineering (Megatron-LM) matters so much.

2.2 Data

Modern pre-training corpora are 10–15 trillion tokens, curated from web crawls (Common Crawl), books, code (GitHub), scientific papers, and curated datasets. Data quality dominates model quality — the field has shifted from "more data" to "better data" (see: Llama-3's emphasis on filtering and deduplication, Llama 3 paper, Section 3).

Key data engineering concerns:

  • Deduplication — exact and near-duplicate removal (MinHash, suffix arrays)
  • Quality filtering — perplexity-based scoring, classifier-based filtering
  • Data mixing — ratio of web/code/books/scientific text matters enormously
  • Tokenization — BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding) with vocabulary sizes of 32k–128k tokens
Connection to your work: This is analogous to your data pipeline work with NVIDIA DALI and WebDataset — but at a much larger scale. The bottleneck is often I/O and preprocessing, not GPU compute. NVIDIA's approach uses NeMo Data Curator for this stage.

2.3 Parallelism Strategies (Megatron-LM)

A 70B-parameter model in FP16 requires ~140 GB just for weights — it doesn't fit on a single 80 GB GPU. A 405B model needs ~810 GB. This necessitates model parallelism beyond what DDP provides. Megatron-LM implements a multi-dimensional parallelism strategy:

Parallelism What's Split Communication When to Use
Data Parallel (DP) Batch across replicas All-reduce gradients Always (outer dimension)
Tensor Parallel (TP) Weight matrices within a layer All-reduce per layer (intra-node) Within a single node (NVLink bandwidth)
Pipeline Parallel (PP) Layers across GPUs Point-to-point (inter-node OK) When TP alone can't fit the model
Sequence Parallel (SP) Sequence dim for LayerNorm/Dropout Minimal (reuses TP comm group) Reduces activation memory
Context Parallel (CP) Sequence dim for attention All-to-all within CP group Very long sequences (128k+ tokens)
Expert Parallel (EP) MoE experts across GPUs All-to-all for token routing Mixture-of-Experts models

In practice, these are composed: e.g., Llama-3 405B training used TP=8 (within node) × PP=16 (across nodes) × DP=many. The total GPU count for frontier models is 16,000–32,000 H100s.

TP vs. FSDP/ZeRO

You know FSDP2 (ZeRO-3), which shards weights/gradients/optimizer states across GPUs but reconstructs them for computation. This is memory-efficient data parallelism — each GPU still processes the full model per forward pass. Tensor Parallelism is fundamentally different: each GPU only ever computes a shard of each layer. TP requires much higher bandwidth (NVLink) but achieves true model parallelism without reconstruction overhead.

In Megatron-LM, you typically use TP within a node (where NVLink gives 900 GB/s) and DP/PP across nodes (where InfiniBand gives ~400 GB/s per link). FSDP is an alternative to Megatron's DP but Megatron's distributed optimizer achieves similar memory savings with better overlap.
Your leverage point: Your experience with DDP/FSDP2/NCCL and scaling to 32 GPUs / 8 nodes maps directly to understanding these parallelism strategies. The difference is degree (16k GPUs vs. 32) and dimensionality (5D parallelism vs. data-parallel only). When the speaker mentions Megatron-LM parallelism, you can map it to your mental model of collective operations and bandwidth constraints.

2.4 Training Infrastructure Details

Key engineering concerns during pre-training that NVIDIA solves:

  • Checkpointing: Distributed checkpointing at scale (TBs of state). NeMo uses async checkpointing to avoid blocking training.
  • Fault tolerance: At 16k GPUs, hardware failures happen hourly. Elastic training, automatic restart from checkpoint.
  • Mixed precision: BF16 for computation, FP32 master weights, loss scaling. On Hopper: FP8 forward pass (E4M3) with FP8 gradients (E5M2) via Transformer Engine.
  • Communication overlap: Overlapping gradient all-reduce with backward pass computation (you already do this with DDP — Megatron extends it to TP/PP communication).
  • Activation checkpointing: Selective recomputation of activations to reduce memory at the cost of ~33% more compute.

2.5 Scaling Laws

Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) established that for compute-optimal training, model size and dataset size should scale equally. For a compute budget C, optimal model size N and token count D satisfy: N ∝ C^0.5, D ∝ C^0.5.

However, the field has moved beyond strict Chinchilla-optimality toward inference-optimal training: deliberately over-training smaller models (more tokens than Chinchilla suggests) because a smaller model is cheaper to serve. Llama-3 8B was trained on 15T tokens — far beyond Chinchilla-optimal — because inference cost dominates total cost of ownership.

Why This Matters for Your Role

The JR1980206 role involves profiling training runs. Understanding scaling laws tells you what's expected performance: if you're profiling a 70B model on 256 GPUs and the MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization) is below 40%, you know something is wrong. Frontier models achieve 40–55% MFU on H100 clusters. This is the performance target.

3. Stage 2: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

After pre-training, the base model is a raw completion engine. SFT transforms it into an instruction-following assistant by training on curated (instruction, response) pairs: 

# SFT data format (simplified)
{"instruction": "Summarize the following article in 3 bullet points.",
 "input": "The European Central Bank announced...",
 "output": "• ECB raised rates by 25bp\n• Inflation forecast revised to...\n• Markets reacted..."}

SFT is standard supervised learning — same cross-entropy loss as pre-training, but only computed on the response tokens (the instruction tokens are masked from the loss). Dataset sizes are small: 10k–100k high-quality examples are sufficient.

Key engineering points:

  • Learning rate is much lower than pre-training (typically 1e-5 to 2e-5)
  • Training is short: 1–5 epochs over the SFT dataset
  • Data quality > quantity: a single bad example can teach harmful patterns
  • Chat template formatting matters (special tokens for turns: <|user|>, <|assistant|>)

SFT vs. Full Fine-tuning vs. LoRA

SFT can be done as full fine-tuning (all parameters updated) or parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA/QLoRA — only low-rank adapter weights updated). At NVIDIA's scale, they typically do full fine-tuning because they have the compute. LoRA is more relevant for customers fine-tuning on limited hardware. NeMo supports both.

4. Stage 3: Alignment (RLHF and DPO)

SFT gets you an instruction-following model, but it still has problems: it may be verbose, hallucinate confidently, produce harmful content, or fail to refuse unsafe requests. Alignment steers the model's behavior toward human preferences beyond just "follow instructions correctly."

4.1 RLHF: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

The classic RLHF pipeline (InstructGPT, Ouyang et al., 2022) has three stages:

Step 1: Train a Reward Model (RM)

Human annotators rank multiple model outputs for the same prompt (e.g., "Response A is better than Response B"). A reward model is trained to predict these rankings: 

# Bradley-Terry preference model
# Given prompt x, preferred response y_w, rejected response y_l:
L_RM = -log(σ(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)))

# Where r(x, y) is the scalar reward score
# σ is the sigmoid function
# This loss pushes r(preferred) > r(rejected)

The reward model is typically initialized from the SFT model (same architecture, but with the language modeling head replaced by a scalar output head).

Step 2: Optimize Policy with PPO

The SFT model is the initial "policy" (in RL terminology). We optimize it to maximize reward while staying close to the SFT model (via a KL divergence penalty): 

# PPO objective for LLM alignment
maximize  E[r(x, y)] - β · KL(π_θ || π_SFT)

# π_θ: current policy (model being optimized)
# π_SFT: reference policy (SFT model, frozen)
# β: KL penalty coefficient
# r(x, y): reward model score

# The KL term prevents "reward hacking" — the model finding
# degenerate outputs that score high on the RM but are nonsensical

PPO is computationally expensive because it requires:

  • 4 models in memory simultaneously: policy, reference policy, reward model, value function (critic)
  • Multiple forward passes per training step (generation + scoring + value estimation)
  • Careful hyperparameter tuning (clipping ratio, GAE λ, minibatch size)

Why RLHF is Hard (Engineering Perspective)

RLHF is an online algorithm: the model generates new outputs, they get scored, and gradients are computed — all within one training step. This creates a complex dependency between generation (autoregressive, memory-bound) and training (compute-bound). It's much harder to keep GPUs utilized compared to standard supervised training. This is where NVIDIA's NeMo-Aligner comes in — it handles the orchestration of these interleaved generation and training phases.

4.2 DPO: Direct Preference Optimization

Rafailov et al. (2023) showed you can skip the reward model entirely. The key insight: the optimal policy under the RLHF objective has a closed-form relationship to the reward function. This lets you reparameterize the reward in terms of the policy itself: 

# DPO loss (directly on preference pairs)
L_DPO = -log σ(β · [log(π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) - log(π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x))])

# No reward model needed!
# π_θ: policy being trained
# π_ref: reference policy (SFT model, frozen)
# y_w: preferred response, y_l: rejected response
# β: temperature parameter

Advantages of DPO over RLHF:

AspectRLHF (PPO)DPO
Models in memory4 (policy, ref, RM, critic)2 (policy, ref)
Training stabilitySensitive to hyperparamsMuch more stable
Compute costHigh (online generation)Lower (offline, like SFT)
Data requirementsCan generate new data onlineFixed preference dataset
Performance ceilingPotentially higher (explores)Limited by offline data quality

Industry State (2025–2026)

In practice, most labs now use DPO variants (or hybrid approaches) rather than pure PPO. Notable developments:
  • GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, DeepSeek, 2024) — removes the need for a separate critic model by using group-level reward baselines
  • Online DPO / Iterative DPO — generates new preference data each iteration, combining DPO's simplicity with PPO's exploration
  • RLVR (RL with Verifiable Rewards) — for math/code, use automated verification instead of human preference (no RM at all)
  • Constitutional AI (Anthropic) — self-critique against principles, generating preference data without humans
Fiameni will likely mention DPO as the practical default and RLHF/PPO as the conceptual foundation.

6. Open Problems & Frontier (Where Good Questions Live)

These are areas where the field is actively unsettled — asking about these signals that you're tracking current research:

  • Alignment tax on capabilities: RLHF/DPO can degrade raw capability (especially on benchmarks). How much alignment is "too much"? Is there a Pareto frontier?
  • Reward hacking at scale: Even with KL constraints, models find degenerate solutions to reward models. Scaling RL makes this worse.
  • Synthetic data for alignment: Can you replace human annotators entirely? Constitutional AI and self-play (SPIN) suggest yes, but quality ceiling is debated.
  • Multi-objective alignment: Helpfulness vs. safety vs. honesty are often in tension. How do you optimize for all three without mode collapse?
  • Scaling post-training compute: DeepSeek-R1 showed that scaling RL compute (test-time and training-time) unlocks reasoning. Is this the future of alignment?
