Three ideas that do most of the work:
  1. Scale invariance. LN(αx) = LN(x). So gradients are orthogonal to the incoming weights, they scale like 1/‖W‖, and the effective learning rate becomes η/‖W‖².
  2. The backward pass is a projection scaled by 1/σ. The input gradient sums to zero and is orthogonal to x − μ.
  3. Computing the statistics is a numerics problem. The naive formula E[x²] − E[x]² cancels catastrophically, and squaring in FP16 overflows above 256.

[verify] marks claims from memory. No external sources were retrieved for this lesson.

1. What normalization buys (and what it does not)

  • The forward scale no longer depends on the init. A layer feeding a norm can have any weight scale. This removes the compounding problem from Lesson 7 for that layer, but not for the residual sum, the embeddings or the output head.
  • Scale invariance changes the optimization geometry (Section 4.3). Weight norm becomes a hidden learning-rate knob, and weight decay controls it.
  • Why BN works is still debated. The original "internal covariate shift" explanation is disputed. Santurkar et al. argue that the main effect is a smoother loss landscape [verify]. In an interview, say "the mechanism is debated" rather than repeating the ICS story as fact.
Normalization fixes the scale of activations, not their direction or rank. Dead ReLUs, collapsed representations and the growth of the residual stream all survive it.

3. Forward definitions

LN: μ = (1/n)Σxi,   σ² = (1/n)Σ(xi − μ)²,   ŷ = (x − μ)/√(σ² + ε),   y = γ ⊙ ŷ + β
RMSNorm: r = √((1/n)Σxi² + ε),   ŷ = x / r,   y = g ⊙ ŷ
BN (train): the same as LN, but μ and σ² are taken over (N, H, W) for each channel. BN (eval): the running μ and σ² are used instead.
  • Biased variance. The normalization uses the biased (1/n) variance. BN's running variance is updated with the unbiased batch variance [verify in your PyTorch version].
  • Where ε goes. Placing ε inside the square root, as above, is the common convention. Some implementations use 1/(σ + ε). The two are not identical when σ is near 0, and this matters when porting checkpoints.
  • Typical ε values:
    • 1e-5 is the default for nn.LayerNorm and BN.
    • 1e-5 or 1e-6 is common in LLM RMSNorm configs [verify].
    • torch.nn.RMSNorm defaults to eps=None, which means torch.finfo(x.dtype).eps. That is 2−7 ≈ 0.0078 for BF16 input, which is not negligible [verify].
  • What RMSNorm gives up. It drops re-centering. You lose invariance to a common shift of the inputs and gain one fewer reduction. Zhang & Sennrich report comparable quality at lower cost [verify numbers].

4. Backward: the Jacobian and its consequences

4.1 Derivation (LN, without γ and β)

Let s = √(σ² + ε). The partial derivatives of the statistics are:

∂μ/∂xj = 1/n,    ∂s/∂xj = (xj − μ)/(n·s) = ŷj/n

Apply the quotient rule to ŷi = (xi − μ)/s:

∂ŷi/∂xj = (δij − 1/n)/s − ((xi − μ)/s²)·(ŷj/n)

Since (xi − μ)/s = ŷi, both terms share the factor 1/s:

J = (1/s) · (I − (1/n)·11ᵀ − (1/n)·ŷŷᵀ)

For RMSNorm, drop the 11ᵀ term: J = (1/r)(I − (1/n)ŷŷᵀ).

4.2 The backward formula you implement

Let ĝ = γ ⊙ gy, where gy = ∂L/∂y. J is symmetric, so:

gx = (1/s) · (ĝ − mean(ĝ) − ŷ · mean(ĝ ⊙ ŷ))
RMSNorm: gx = (1/r) · (ĝ − ŷ · mean(ĝ ⊙ ŷ))
Parameter gradients: dγ = Σtokens gy ⊙ ŷ,   dβ = Σtokens gy

Two consequences:

  • The input gradient is projected. J annihilates 1 (shift invariance) and, up to ε, also annihilates ŷ (scale invariance). So gx sums to zero and is orthogonal to x − μ.
  • The gain is 1/s. The spectral norm of J is at most 1/s. If a large residual stream (pre-LN, Section 6) feeds the norm, the gradient into the stream is attenuated by 1/rms(stream).

4.3 Scale invariance and the effective learning rate

Let W feed a norm, so L(αW) = L(W) for α > 0. Differentiate with respect to α at α = 1:

⟨∇WL, W⟩ = 0,    ∇L(αW) = (1/α) ∇L(W)

Now take a plain SGD step w′ = w − ηg. Because g ⊥ w:

‖w′‖² = ‖w‖² + η²‖g‖²  ⇒  the norm only grows

The angle of the update is η‖g‖/‖w‖. Since ‖g‖ ∝ 1/‖w‖, it follows that:

ηeff ∝ η / ‖w‖²
  • Without weight decay, ‖w‖ grows and the effective learning rate decays on its own.
  • Weight decay shrinks ‖w‖, which raises ηeff. The two forces reach an equilibrium. This is why weight decay "matters" even though it cannot change the function the network computes [verify: van Laarhoven 2017].
  • With Adam, the update magnitude is roughly η regardless of ‖g‖, so the angular step scales like η/‖w‖ instead.

5. Numerics of the statistics (links to Lessons 1 and 3)

5.1 Catastrophic cancellation in E[x²] − E[x]²

Let u be the unit roundoff and write E[x²] = μ² + σ². Computing E[x²] in floating point introduces an absolute error of about u·(μ² + σ²) ≈ u·μ². The result is garbage once this error is comparable to σ²:

Failure when μ/σ ≳ u−1/2:   FP32 (u = 2−24) → 4096,   FP16 (2−11) → ~45,   BF16 (2−9) → ~22

This is a lower bound. Accumulating over n terms makes it worse. When the result becomes negative, rsqrt(var + ε) returns NaN. Realistic triggers:

  • Uncentered inputs such as raw pixels in 0–255, sensor offsets or timestamps.
  • BN on channels with a large DC component.
  • Any statistics accumulated in BF16.

5.2 The fix: two-pass, Welford, and the parallel merge

Center first (two-pass), or use Welford's online update. GPU kernels reduce per thread, warp or block and then merge partial (n, μ, M2) triples with the Chan et al. formula:

n = na + nb,   δ = μb − μa,   μ = μa + δ·nb/n,   M2 = M2a + M2b + δ²·nanb/n,   Var = M2/n

Every term in the merge is a sum of non-negative quantities. Nothing large is subtracted, so there is no cancellation.

5.3 RMSNorm in FP16: squaring overflows

The FP16 maximum is 65504, and √65504 ≈ 255.9. Any activation with |x| ≥ 256 gives x² = inf. Then the mean is inf, rsqrt(inf) = 0, and the whole token is silently set to zero. There is no NaN and no alarm. Large-magnitude outlier dimensions in LLM residual streams make this realistic [verify: "massive activations", Sun et al. 2024].

The fix is to upcast to FP32 before squaring, as reference LLaMA-style implementations do [verify], and to cast the output back.

Subtlety: x.pow(2).mean() on an FP16 tensor may accumulate internally in FP32 and survive. .sum() overflows once the total exceeds 65504. The elementwise square overflows regardless of what accumulator the reduction uses.

5.4 Mixed precision in practice

  • Autocast: PyTorch autocast puts layer_norm and group_norm on its FP32 list, so the output dtype may be FP32 [verify in your version]. cuDNN BN accepts FP16 inputs with FP32 parameters and statistics [verify].
  • Fused kernels (Apex, Transformer Engine, Triton) read BF16 or FP16, accumulate statistics in FP32 and write BF16 or FP16. Save mean and rstd in FP32 for the backward pass.
  • ε in low precision: 1e-5 and 1e-6 are FP16 subnormals (the minimum normal is 6.1e-5). Add ε in FP32.
  • γ: γ is a parameter like any other. Keep it in FP32 or master precision (Lesson 2).

6. Pre-LN vs post-LN

Post-LN: xl+1 = LN(xl + F(xl))   |   Pre-LN: xl+1 = xl + F(LN(xl)), with a final LN before the head
Post-LN (original Transformer)Pre-LN (GPT-2 onward [verify])
Stream scale at initFixed at ≈ 1 after every blockGrows: Var(xL) ≈ Var(x0) + L·c (Lesson 7)
Gradient pathNo pure identity path. Every block's gradient goes through an LN Jacobian.Exact identity term: ∂xL/∂xl = I + (branch terms)
WarmupTypically required. Xiong et al.: gradients near the output are large at init [verify]Often trainable with little or no warmup [verify]
Typical failureEarly divergence; sensitive to learning rateLater blocks contribute relatively little (ratio ‖F‖/‖x‖ ≈ 1/√l); stream magnitudes and outliers grow
Quality when both trainOften reported as equal or better [verify]Robust default at scale

Variants worth naming:

  • DeepNorm: LN(α·x + F(x)) with α > 1 and down-scaled branch init [verify].
  • Admin: Liu et al. argue that post-LN instability comes from amplified dependence on the residual branches [verify].
  • Sandwich-LN.
  • QK-norm: LN applied to q and k so that attention logits cannot grow. This is the same mechanism as √dk from Lesson 7, but enforced during training [verify: ViT-22B].
A link interviewers like: in pre-LN, each block's norm divides by rms(xl) ≈ √l. Both the forward contribution and the gradient through that branch are therefore scaled down with depth. Residual scaling such as 1/√(2N) and zero-init of the output projections (Lesson 7) change this trade-off.

7. BatchNorm in production

7.1 Running statistics

  • PyTorch's momentum convention: running = (1 − m)·running + m·batch, with default m = 0.1. Keras and TF use the opposite convention, with momentum ≈ 0.99 as the decay [verify]. momentum=None gives a cumulative average.
  • Small batches: per-GPU batches of 1–4 give noisy statistics. Fixes:
    • SyncBN, which combines per-rank mean, variance and count, not raw sums of squares.
    • GroupNorm.
    • Frozen BN statistics during fine-tuning.
    With N = 1 and no spatial dimensions, BatchNorm1d raises an error in training mode.
  • DDP: by default, buffers (the running statistics) are broadcast from rank 0 [verify: broadcast_buffers=True]. Without SyncBN, each rank normalizes with its local batch statistics.
  • Batch coupling: samples in a batch influence each other. This can leak information, for example in contrastive learning, which is why MoCo uses shuffle-BN [verify].

7.2 Folding BN into the preceding conv (inference)

Let sc be the per-channel scale:

sc = γc / √(σ²run,c + ε),    W′c = sc · Wc,    b′c = sc·(bc − μrun,c) + βc

TensorRT and similar compilers fold BN automatically. The numerics trap:

  • A nearly dead channel with a tiny σ²run gets an enormous sc.
  • Under per-tensor INT8 weight quantization, that one channel sets the scale, and most other weights round to 0.
  • Folded weights therefore need per-channel weight quantization. Also check the FP16 range of W′.

This is the BN version of the outlier story from SmoothQuant (Lesson 10).

7.3 "Great in train mode, bad in eval mode": checklist

  1. model.eval() was called, or forgotten, or a submodule was put back into train mode.
  2. Running statistics are stale or were computed under different preprocessing.
  3. The momentum convention was mixed up when porting from TF.
  4. Small per-GPU batches were used without SyncBN.
  5. The BN statistics were frozen, or should have been, during fine-tuning.

8. Systems angle (Nvidia/Helsing relevance)

8.1 Normalization is memory-bound

The LN forward pass in BF16 reads 2 B and writes 2 B per element and does ~8 FLOPs. That is an arithmetic intensity of ~2 FLOP/B, far below the ridge point of H100-class GPUs (a few hundred FLOP/B [verify specs]). So the goal is to minimize memory traffic.

8.2 Fuse add and norm

  • Unfused: the add reads x and F(x) and writes x′; the norm then reads x′ and writes y. That is 5 tensor passes.
  • Fused: read x and F(x), write x′ and y. That is 4 passes, about 20% less traffic.

Residual-add + dropout + LN fusions in Apex and Transformer Engine exist for exactly this reason.

8.3 One pass vs two passes

If a row with d ≲ 8–16K elements fits in registers or shared memory, a two-pass computation on chip is essentially free and numerically safe. Single-pass Welford matters when the row must be streamed from memory.

8.4 Backward-pass reductions and determinism

  • dγ and dβ are column reductions over all tokens.
  • Implementations either compute partial sums per block followed by a second-stage reduction (deterministic), or use atomics (fast, non-deterministic, see Lesson 3).
  • Save mean and rstd per row in FP32 (8 B per row) rather than recomputing them.

8.5 Deployment

TensorRT has warned about LayerNorm accuracy in FP16 and recommends keeping norm layers in FP32 [verify version]. The cause is the overflow in 5.3 and the cancellation in 5.1, now at inference time.

8.6 Step-0 health check, extended from Lesson 7

For each block, log:

  • rms(stream)
  • rms(F)/rms(stream)
  • ‖grad‖ of the branch weights
  • min/max of σ² inside every norm

Values of σ² near ε, or max |x| ≥ 256 in FP16 paths, are red flags.

9. Lab (~45 min)

Write down a prediction for each printed line before running. CPU is sufficient. Part 2 needs FP16 ops on CPU (recent PyTorch), so use .cuda() if they are unsupported.

import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)

# ---------------- Part 1: catastrophic cancellation in the variance ----------------
def var_naive(x):                      # E[x^2] - E[x]^2, single pass
    return (x * x).mean() - x.mean() ** 2

def merge(a, b):                       # Chan et al. parallel merge of (n, mean, M2)
    na, ma, M2a = a
    nb, mb, M2b = b
    n = na + nb
    d = mb - ma
    return n, ma + d * (nb / n), M2a + M2b + d * d * (na * nb / n)

def var_welford_tree(x, chunk=256):    # per-"block" two-pass, then tree merge (like a GPU kernel)
    stats = []
    for c in x.split(chunk):
        m = c.mean()
        stats.append((c.numel(), m, ((c - m) ** 2).sum()))
    while len(stats) > 1:
        stats = [merge(stats[i], stats[i + 1]) if i + 1 < len(stats) else stats[i]
                 for i in range(0, len(stats), 2)]
    n, _, M2 = stats[0]
    return M2 / n

x = 1e4 + 0.01 * torch.randn(65536)    # mean/std = 1e6, far above FP32's ~4096 threshold
vn, vw = var_naive(x), var_welford_tree(x)
print(f"naive={vn.item():.6g}  welford-tree={vw.item():.6g}  torch.var={x.var(unbiased=False).item():.6g}")
print("rsqrt(naive + eps):", torch.rsqrt(vn + 1e-5).item())

# ---------------- Part 2: RMSNorm in FP16 ----------------
d = 4096
h = (5 * torch.randn(d)).half()
print("\nfp16 sum of squares :", h.pow(2).sum().item())
print("fp16 mean of squares:", h.pow(2).mean().item())
h[0] = 300.0                                         # one outlier dimension
ms16 = h.pow(2).mean()
y16 = h * torch.rsqrt(ms16 + 1e-6)
ms32 = h.float().pow(2).mean()
y32 = (h.float() * torch.rsqrt(ms32 + 1e-6)).half()
print(f"ms16={ms16.item()}  max|y16|={y16.abs().max().item()}  "
      f"nan={torch.isnan(y16).any().item()}  max|y32|={y32.abs().max().item():.2f}")

# ---------------- Part 3: scale invariance of LN ----------------
W0 = torch.randn(256, 256) / 16
xin = torch.randn(64, 256)
ln = nn.LayerNorm(256)
for s in (1.0, 10.0):
    W = (s * W0).requires_grad_()
    ln(xin @ W.T).pow(3).mean().backward()           # arbitrary non-trivial loss
    cos = (W.grad * W).sum() / (W.grad.norm() * W.norm())
    print(f"scale={s:4.0f}  |grad|={W.grad.norm().item():.4e}  cos(grad, W)={cos.item():+.1e}")

# ---------------- Part 4: pre-LN vs post-LN at init ----------------
class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, pre):
        super().__init__()
        self.pre, self.ln = pre, nn.LayerNorm(d)
        self.fc1, self.fc2 = nn.Linear(d, 4 * d), nn.Linear(4 * d, d)
        nn.init.kaiming_normal_(self.fc1.weight, nonlinearity="relu")
        nn.init.normal_(self.fc2.weight, 0.0, (4 * d) ** -0.5)   # branch F is variance-preserving
        nn.init.zeros_(self.fc1.bias)
        nn.init.zeros_(self.fc2.bias)

    def f(self, x):
        return self.fc2(torch.relu(self.fc1(x)))

    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.f(self.ln(x)) if self.pre else self.ln(x + self.f(x))

def run(pre, depth=24, d=256, batch=128):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    blocks = nn.ModuleList(Block(d, pre) for _ in range(depth))
    x0 = torch.randn(batch, d, requires_grad=True)
    h, rms, ratio = x0, [], []
    for blk in blocks:
        with torch.no_grad():
            inp = blk.ln(h) if pre else h
            ratio.append((blk.f(inp).pow(2).mean().sqrt() / h.pow(2).mean().sqrt()).item())
        h = blk(h)
        rms.append(h.pow(2).mean().sqrt().item())
    out = nn.LayerNorm(d)(h) if pre else h
    out.backward(torch.randn_like(out))
    g = [blk.fc1.weight.grad.norm().item() for blk in blocks]
    return rms, ratio, g, x0.grad.norm().item()

for pre in (True, False):
    rms, ratio, g, gx = run(pre)
    tag = "pre-LN " if pre else "post-LN"
    print(f"\n{tag} stream RMS  b1/6/12/24: {rms[0]:.2f} {rms[5]:.2f} {rms[11]:.2f} {rms[23]:.2f}")
    print(f"{tag} |F|/|x|     b1/6/12/24: {ratio[0]:.2f} {ratio[5]:.2f} {ratio[11]:.2f} {ratio[23]:.2f}")
    print(f"{tag} |dW_fc1|    b1/12/24  : {g[0]:.3e} {g[11]:.3e} {g[23]:.3e}   |dx0|={gx:.3e}")

# ---------------- Part 5: BN folding and INT8 ----------------
conv = nn.Conv2d(16, 32, 3, padding=1, bias=False)
bn = nn.BatchNorm2d(32, momentum=None)               # cumulative running average
with torch.no_grad():
    for _ in range(20):
        bn(conv(torch.randn(8, 16, 16, 16)))
bn.eval()

def fold(conv, bn):
    s = bn.weight / torch.sqrt(bn.running_var + bn.eps)
    return conv.weight * s[:, None, None, None], bn.bias - bn.running_mean * s

def zero_frac(W, per_channel):
    amax = W.abs().amax(dim=(1, 2, 3), keepdim=True) if per_channel else W.abs().max()
    return ((W / (amax / 127)).round() == 0).float().mean().item()

with torch.no_grad():
    xb = torch.randn(4, 16, 16, 16)
    Wf, bf = fold(conv, bn)
    print("\nfold max abs err:", (bn(conv(xb)) - F.conv2d(xb, Wf, bf, padding=1)).abs().max().item())
    bn.running_var[0] = 1e-6                         # a nearly dead channel
    Wf, bf = fold(conv, bn)
    print(f"INT8 zero fraction  per-tensor={zero_frac(Wf, False):.1%}  per-channel={zero_frac(Wf, True):.2%}")
Expected behavior (open after running)

Part 1

  • naive is garbage: typically a small multiple of 8 (the FP32 spacing near 108), possibly 0 or negative.
  • If it is negative, rsqrt returns NaN.
  • welford-tree and torch.var both give ≈ 1.0e-4.

Part 2

  • Sum of squares: inf (the expected total is ~102400, above 65504).
  • Mean of squares: ≈ 25, if the reduction accumulates in FP32 internally [verify on your device].
  • With the outlier, 300² = 90000 overflows: ms16 = inf, max|y16| = 0, nan = False. The token is silently zeroed.
  • The FP32 path gives max |y| ≈ 300/6.85 ≈ 43.8.

Part 3

  • The gradient norm drops by exactly 10× at scale 10.
  • |cos(grad, W)| ≲ 1e-4. It is not exactly 0 only because of ε.

Part 4: firm predictions

  • Pre-LN stream RMS ≈ √(l+1): ≈ 1.41, 2.65, 3.61, 5.0.
  • Pre-LN |F|/|x| ≈ 1/√l: ≈ 1.0, 0.41, 0.29, 0.20.
  • Post-LN stream RMS ≈ 1.00 everywhere. Its |F|/|x| stays ≈ 1.

Part 4: measure, do not overclaim

  • Record the gradient profiles and compare them with Xiong et al.'s full-transformer analysis.
  • A pure MLP toy may show much milder differences than a full transformer. Being able to say "the theory depends on the block structure; here is what I measured" is the interview skill.

Part 5

  • Fold error ≈ 1e-6.
  • After killing channel 0: per-tensor zero fraction ≈ ⅔, per-channel ≈ 0.4%.

Stretch tasks:

  1. Implement the LN backward formula from 4.2 by hand and compare it with autograd using torch.autograd.gradcheck in FP64.
  2. In Part 4, scale fc2 by 1/√(2·depth) and observe the stream growth flatten.

10. Interview drills (~15 min)

10.1 Thirty-second answer

"Normalization rescales activations with statistics. BN computes them per channel over the batch, LN and RMSNorm per token over the features. The key property is scale invariance: LN(αWx) = LN(Wx). So gradients are orthogonal to W and scale like 1/‖W‖, the effective learning rate is η/‖W‖², and weight decay acts as a learning-rate controller. The backward pass is a projection scaled by 1/σ. Numerically, I compute the statistics in FP32 with a two-pass or Welford merge, because E[x²] − E[x]² fails once μ/σ exceeds about u−1/2. For RMSNorm I upcast before squaring, because x² overflows FP16 above 256 and silently zeroes tokens. Architecturally, pre-LN gives an exact identity gradient path and needs little warmup, but its stream grows. Post-LN keeps the scale fixed but needs warmup. For BN deployment I fold into the conv and use per-channel weight quantization."

10.2 Whiteboard prompts

Derive the LayerNorm input gradient. What two properties does it have?

J = (1/s)(I − 11ᵀ/n − ŷŷᵀ/n), which gives gx = (1/s)(ĝ − mean(ĝ) − ŷ·mean(ĝ⊙ŷ)). The gradient sums to zero (shift invariance) and is orthogonal to x − μ, up to ε (scale invariance). Its gain is at most 1/s.

A LLaMA-style model in FP16 inference outputs zero vectors for some tokens. There is no NaN. Diagnose.

RMSNorm is computed in FP16. An outlier with |x| ≥ 256 squares to inf, so rsqrt(inf) = 0 and the token becomes zeros. To confirm, log max |x| before each norm. The fix is to upcast to FP32 before squaring and add ε in FP32. The fused-kernel equivalent is FP32 accumulation.

Why does weight decay matter for weights followed by a norm, given that it cannot change the function?

The gradient is orthogonal to w, so SGD steps only grow ‖w‖, and ηeff ∝ η/‖w‖² decays. Weight decay shrinks ‖w‖ and so sets the equilibrium effective learning rate. It works as a schedule knob, not as a regularizer of the function.

A post-LN transformer diverges in the first 1k steps. What are your options?

In order of effort:

  1. Learning-rate warmup or a lower peak learning rate.
  2. Switch to pre-LN with a final LN, scaling the residual branches.
  3. DeepNorm or Admin-style residual reweighting.
  4. QK-norm if the attention logits grow.

Also check:

  • Gradient-norm spikes per layer.
  • Whether the loss scaler is skipping steps (Lesson 2).
INT8 PTQ of a ResNet-style model loses 10 points. FP16 is fine. What do you check first?
  • BN-folded weights quantized per tensor, combined with channels whose tiny running variance makes sc = γ/√(σ²+ε) huge.
  • Compare per-channel amax distributions. Switch to per-channel weight scales.
  • Inspect or prune dead channels.
  • Then look at the activation calibration (Lesson 10).
When is the single-pass E[x²] − E[x]² acceptable in a kernel?

When it is accumulated in FP32 and μ/σ ≪ 4096 is guaranteed, which is rarely provable for arbitrary inputs. If the row fits on chip, a two-pass computation from registers or shared memory costs almost nothing, so it is usually the default. For streaming rows, use a Welford merge.
