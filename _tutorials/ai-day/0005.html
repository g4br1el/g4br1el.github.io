Q1.1 TIER 1 GPU Utilization in RLHF

"The RLHF stage has fundamentally different compute characteristics from pre-training — it interleaves autoregressive generation, which is memory-bandwidth-bound, with gradient updates, which are compute-bound. How does NeMo-Aligner handle GPU utilization during PPO training? Do you overlap generation with backward passes of other samples, or is there an inherent pipeline bubble?"

"Great question. Yes, this is one of the hardest systems challenges in alignment. In NeMo-Aligner, we actually separate the generation phase and the training phase into distinct steps rather than trying to overlap them — so there IS a utilization gap when switching between modes. We mitigate it by increasing the batch size during generation and by using tensor parallelism to reduce per-token latency. In newer versions, we're exploring asynchronous generation where a separate set of workers generates rollouts while the training workers process previous batches. But it's an active area — utilization during PPO is typically 30–40% lower than during pre-training."
"That's interesting — so the 30–40% utilization drop is essentially the 'alignment tax' at the infrastructure level, not just the model level. Have you measured whether switching to DPO recovers most of that utilization, since it eliminates the online generation phase entirely?"

This follow-up is low-risk and shows you immediately grasped the implication. It also pivots to DPO, which they're likely promoting.

Q1.2 TIER 1 Megatron vs. FSDP at Scale

"I use FSDP2 with NCCL for distributed training on HPC clusters — scaling to 32 GPUs across 8 nodes. Megatron-LM's approach with tensor and pipeline parallelism is architecturally very different. At what scale does the Megatron approach become necessary over FSDP? Is there a model-size or cluster-size threshold where the communication overhead of FSDP's all-gather becomes prohibitive?"

"It depends on whether the model fits in a single GPU's memory. FSDP is excellent when the model fits — or nearly fits — per GPU after sharding optimizer state. The transition point is roughly when you need true model parallelism: the model is so large that even with ZeRO-3 sharding, you can't fit a reasonable micro-batch. For Llama-3 70B, you could use FSDP on 8 H100s, but you'd have very small micro-batches. With Megatron's TP=8 within a node, each GPU only holds 1/8 of each layer, so you can fit larger micro-batches and get better compute efficiency. At 405B, there's no question — you need Megatron-style parallelism. The threshold is usually around 30–40B parameters on H100 80GB nodes."
"Makes sense. So for the 30–40B range, do customers typically start with FSDP and migrate to Megatron when they need to scale further, or is NeMo's recommendation to start with Megatron from the beginning?"

This follow-up positions you as a potential customer/user — exactly the person this role would support.

Q1.3 TIER 2 FP8 Pre-training at Scale

"You mentioned Transformer Engine for FP8 training on Hopper. When running 5D parallelism at scale, does the per-tensor FP8 scaling introduce synchronization overhead in the tensor-parallel communication? Specifically — after a TP all-reduce, do the scaling factors need to be re-computed for the fused tensor, or are they handled independently per shard?"

"The scaling factors are computed per-tensor, where 'tensor' here means the local shard on each GPU. After the TP all-reduce, the result is in higher precision — FP16 or BF16 — and then requantized to FP8 for the next layer's input with a new scaling factor. So the all-reduce itself happens in BF16 — you don't communicate FP8 values across the TP group. The overhead of computing scaling factors is negligible — it's essentially a max-abs reduction that piggybacks on the existing computation. The delayed scaling approach means we use last iteration's scale, so there's no synchronization point for the scale itself."
"Understood — so the TP communication is still in BF16, and FP8 is only used within each GPU's local computation. Does that mean the bandwidth savings of FP8 are mainly on the HBM access side rather than the inter-GPU communication side?"

This shows you're reasoning through where FP8 actually helps vs. where it doesn't. Very strong systems signal.

Talk 2: Inference Optimization — Dr. Séverine Habert

Habert will likely cover: the latency/throughput challenge → quantization (FP8, FP4, AWQ) → KV-cache management → in-flight batching → speculative decoding → maybe disaggregated serving. She may show benchmarks comparing TensorRT-LLM vs. competitors (vLLM, etc.) on throughput/latency curves.

Q2.1 TIER 1 FP4 Shifting the Bottleneck

"With FP4 on Blackwell, weight loading cost drops by 4× versus FP16, which should push the decode phase closer to compute-bound territory — at least for reasonable batch sizes. Does that fundamentally change the optimization strategy? Specifically, does speculative decoding become less beneficial when decode is no longer purely memory-bandwidth-limited?"

"Excellent observation. Yes, with FP4 on Blackwell, at moderate batch sizes you can actually reach the compute-bound regime for decode — which is a regime shift that changes the optimization playbook. Speculative decoding is primarily a latency optimization for the bandwidth-bound regime. When you're compute-bound, the draft model's tokens don't come 'for free' — they compete for the same compute resources. So the benefit diminishes. However, for single-request latency at batch=1, even with FP4 on Blackwell, you're still bandwidth-bound because the arithmetic intensity is so low. The crossover depends on batch size. We're actively studying where that crossover point is for different model sizes on B200."
"That crossover point sounds like exactly what a roofline analysis would reveal — memory bandwidth ceiling versus compute ceiling as a function of batch size and quantization level. Is that how your team characterizes new hardware/model combinations internally?"

This follow-up explicitly names roofline analysis — a core tool for the JR1980206 role — and asks about internal methodology. It invites them to describe the team's workflow, which is intelligence for your application.

Q2.2 TIER 1 CUDA Graphs vs. Dynamic Batching

"In-flight batching changes the active batch size every iteration, which conflicts with CUDA Graphs requiring static shapes. How does TensorRT-LLM handle this in practice — a pool of pre-captured graphs for discrete batch sizes with switching between them? And what's the graph-switching overhead relative to the decode step duration?"

"Yes, TensorRT-LLM maintains a set of CUDA Graphs captured at different 'bucket' batch sizes. When the actual batch size changes, we select the graph with the closest matching size — padding the batch if necessary. The graph switching overhead is minimal — tens of microseconds — because it's just updating the graph's input pointers. The real cost is the padding waste: if your graph buckets are [1, 4, 8, 16, 32] and you have 5 active requests, you run the batch=8 graph with 3 padded dummy entries. We have heuristics for choosing bucket sizes based on traffic patterns. Some deployments disable CUDA Graphs when batch sizes are very dynamic and the padding waste exceeds the launch overhead savings."
"Interesting — so there's a trade-off between graph granularity (more buckets = less padding waste, more memory for graphs) and graph capture cost. Is this something the JR1980206 performance team profiles per-deployment, or is there a universal optimal bucket configuration?"

Directly names the job ID. Shows you've read the posting. Asks about the team's work. Bold but effective.

Q2.3 TIER 2 KV-Cache Quantization Accuracy

"For KV-cache quantization to FP8 or INT8 — how does accuracy degrade for long-context tasks? I'd expect quantization error to accumulate across attention layers as the same quantized KV entries are attended to repeatedly. Do you observe layer-position-dependent degradation, and if so, is there a strategy like mixed-precision KV — full precision for early layers, quantized for later ones?"

"We do see some sensitivity in the first few layers — the attention patterns in early layers tend to have sharper distributions where quantization noise matters more. For most models up to 32k context, FP8 KV-cache shows negligible degradation. Beyond 128k, we see measurable perplexity increase — around 0.1–0.2 points. The mixed-precision approach you suggest is something we've experimented with: keeping the first 2–4 layers in FP16 while quantizing the rest to FP8 gives you 95% of the memory savings with almost no accuracy loss. TensorRT-LLM supports this configuration but it's not the default yet — it requires per-model profiling to determine the right split point."
"The per-model profiling to find the split point — is that something ModelOpt automates, or is it manual experimentation? I ask because at Fraunhofer we faced a similar problem with layer-wise quantization sensitivity for CNNs on FPGA — we ended up building automated sensitivity analysis tooling."

Connects directly to your VitisAI/FPGA work. Shows you've solved analogous problems. Signals you'd contribute immediately to automating this workflow.

Q2.4 TIER 2 Profiling Methodology

"When you're characterizing a new model on new hardware — say, a Mixture-of-Experts model on Blackwell — what's your profiling methodology for identifying the primary bottleneck? Do you use standard Nsight Systems/Compute traces and manually interpret them, or does the team have higher-level tooling that maps kernel-level metrics to application-level metrics like TTFT and TPS?"

"Both. We absolutely use Nsight Systems — it's our ground truth for understanding what's happening at the kernel level. But we also have internal profiling layers within TensorRT-LLM that report per-layer execution time, memory allocation, and communication overhead. For new hardware, we typically start with a roofline analysis: what's the theoretical peak throughput given the model's arithmetic intensity on this hardware? Then we measure actual throughput and trace the gap. The gap analysis tells us where to focus — is it kernel efficiency, memory bandwidth utilization, scheduling overhead, or communication? For MoE models specifically, the expert routing introduces load imbalance that's hard to see in standard traces — we've built custom visualization tools for that."
"The gap analysis approach is very close to how I profile distributed training — measuring theoretical scaling versus actual, then tracing the deficit to communication, memory, or compute. If I joined the team, would the methodology transfer, or is inference profiling a fundamentally different discipline from training profiling?"

Extremely direct — "if I joined the team." Shows confidence and directly asks about transferability of your skills. Only use this if the conversation is warm.

Q2.5 TIER 2 Disaggregated Serving

"I've read about disaggregated serving — separating prefill and decode onto different GPU pools because they have different compute characteristics. Is TensorRT-LLM moving in this direction? It seems like it would require transferring the KV-cache between GPU pools after prefill, which could be expensive over the network."

"Yes, disaggregated serving is something we're actively working on. The KV-cache transfer is the key challenge — for a 70B model with 4k context, you're transferring about 10 GB of KV-cache from the prefill pool to the decode pool. Over InfiniBand at 400 Gb/s, that's about 200ms — which is comparable to the prefill time itself. So the benefit depends on the workload: for very long prompts with short outputs, disaggregation helps because prefill is the bottleneck. For short prompts with long outputs, the transfer overhead may not be worth it. We're looking at techniques like KV-cache streaming — start decode before the full KV-cache transfer is complete."
"The KV-cache streaming idea is elegant — essentially pipelining the transfer with early decode steps that only need the first few KV entries. Does that require changes to the attention kernel to handle partially-available KV-cache, or is it handled at the scheduler level?"

Shows you immediately grasp the implication and think about implementation details. Strong systems signal.

Q2.6 TIER 3 2:4 Sparsity in Practice

"You showed 2:4 structured sparsity giving 2× compute throughput with minimal accuracy loss. In practice, how well does this compose with quantization — can you do FP8 sparse, or FP4 sparse? Is the combined speedup multiplicative (4× from sparsity+quantization), or are there diminishing returns?"

"FP8 with 2:4 sparsity is supported on Hopper and gives close to multiplicative speedup — roughly 3.5–4× versus FP16 dense for GEMM operations. FP4 sparse is theoretically possible on Blackwell but we're still validating accuracy. The challenge is that both sparsity and quantization remove information — combining them aggressively can push below accuracy thresholds for sensitive tasks. For inference on well-trained models, FP8 sparse is very reliable. FP4 sparse is more experimental."

Q3.1 TIER 1 Tokenizer Information Bottleneck

"My research background is in compression and spatial machine learning — specifically how lossy compression affects downstream ML performance. The Cosmos tokenizer applies 512× compression to video. I'm curious: is the tokenizer fidelity currently the accuracy bottleneck for physics prediction, or is it the world model itself? Can the tokenizer distinguish, for instance, between a rigid object sliding and a deformable object deforming?"

"That's a really interesting perspective — connecting compression theory to world models. The short answer is: it depends on the spatial scale. For large-scale dynamics — objects moving, gravity, collisions — the tokenizer preserves enough information. For fine-grained physics like deformation, fabric folding, or fluid splashes, we do see that the tokenizer is the limiting factor. The 8×8 spatial patches can't represent sub-patch deformation well. We're working on hierarchical tokenizers that use coarse tokens for global structure and fine-grained tokens for physics-critical regions. But yes, the tokenizer is often the bottleneck for fine-grained physics — not the world model."
"That's very consistent with what I found in my research — spatial compression below a critical threshold causes abrupt performance drops in downstream tasks, not gradual degradation. Have you characterized that critical threshold for the tokenizer? For example — is 4×4 spatial significantly better, or is there a sweet spot where physics fidelity plateaus?"

Directly references your published findings. Demonstrates research depth. Positions you as someone with unique expertise relevant to their problem.

Q3.2 TIER 1 Training Data Pipeline at Scale

"Training on 20 million hours of video is one of the most demanding data pipeline challenges in ML. I work on GPU-accelerated data pipelines with NVIDIA DALI for image-heavy workloads. What does the Cosmos training data pipeline look like? Is video decoding and tokenization done on-the-fly on GPU, or are tokens pre-computed and stored — and what are the storage implications of pre-tokenization at that scale?"

"It's a hybrid approach. We pre-compute tokens for the most frequently used data — this avoids the cost of repeated video decoding and tokenization during training. But pre-tokenized storage is enormous: even at 512× compression, 20M hours of video generates petabytes of tokens. For less-used data or for data augmentation, we do on-the-fly tokenization using GPU-accelerated video decoding. The pipeline uses DALI for decoding and our custom tokenizer kernels. The I/O subsystem is actually the hardest engineering challenge — you need sustained multi-TB/s read throughput from distributed storage to feed thousands of GPUs."
"Multi-TB/s sustained I/O is exactly the challenge we face in HPC — we use Lustre with WebDataset sharding to keep GPU pipelines fed. At your scale, is it a similar parallel filesystem approach, or have you moved to object storage with prefetching?"

Shows concrete HPC infrastructure knowledge. Signals you understand the systems-level challenge behind the research.

Q3.3 TIER 2 Sim-to-Real + Quantization Compound Gap

"You showed the sim-to-real transfer pipeline for robotics. In my experience deploying quantized models to edge hardware like FPGAs, there's always a deployment accuracy gap. For Physical AI, you have a compounding problem: sim-to-real gap PLUS compression gap when deploying to Jetson Thor. How does NVIDIA handle this — quantization-aware training in simulation, or is the policy robust enough that post-training quantization doesn't degrade control quality?"

"You're right that the gaps compound. In practice, robot control policies are actually quite robust to quantization — they tend to be smaller models (compared to LLMs) and the output space is continuous joint angles, which are tolerant to small numerical errors. We typically do PTQ to INT8 for Jetson deployment and see negligible performance degradation. The sim-to-real gap is much larger than the quantization gap. Where quantization hurts is in the perception stack — visual encoders that feed into the policy. Those require more careful quantization. For GR00T specifically, we quantize-aware-train the vision encoder but PTQ the action decoder."
"That layered approach — QAT for perception, PTQ for control — is elegant. Is TensorRT the deployment engine on Jetson Thor, and if so, does it use the same optimization stack we discussed in the previous talk, or is there a robotics-specific TensorRT configuration?"

Q3.6 TIER 3 Long-Horizon Prediction Drift

"You showed world model predictions over several seconds. How does prediction quality degrade with time horizon? General Strategy: How to Ask Questions Well

Mechanics

  1. Stand up (if seated). It commands attention and the speaker will face you.
  2. State name and affiliation first: "Gabriel Dax, Fraunhofer IIS —" then pause 1 second.
  3. Frame before asking: Give a 1-sentence context so the speaker (and audience) understands where you're coming from. E.g., "I work on profiling distributed training on HPC clusters, so I'm curious about..."
  4. Ask ONE thing. Don't bundle two questions. If your question has "and also" — cut the second part.
  5. Keep it under 20 seconds. Anything longer and you've lost the room.
  6. End with a clear question mark. Don't trail off into a statement. End with "...so my question is: X?"

If You Don't Understand the Answer

Say: "That's helpful, thank you. Let me process that." Do NOT ask a confused follow-up. You can approach the speaker privately during the break for clarification — that's actually better for building a personal connection.

If Someone Else Asks Your Question First

Switch to your backup. If both are taken, use a reactive question that builds on what the speaker just said in response to the other person. Prefix with: "Building on that previous question —"

If There's No Q&A Time

Approach the speaker during the break or after the final talk. Say: "I had a question about your talk if you have a moment —" This is actually BETTER for recruitment because you get a private 1-on-1 conversation rather than a public Q&A exchange.

Body Language

  • Eye contact with the speaker while asking
  • Nod when they're answering (shows you're following)
  • Don't look at notes while asking — memorize the core of your question
  • Smile briefly after they finish (shows you appreciated the answer)
